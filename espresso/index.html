Tamper, funnel, and preparation tools

Choose a flat tamper sized to your basket; a self-leveling design can simplify repeatability. A small dosing funnel makes transfer cleaner. A WDT needle tool can help break up clumps and improve distribution. Neither a calibrated tamper nor anti-static grinder technology guarantees an even coffee bed.

Browse tampers · Browse preparation tools. Check sizing and fit.

Milk, storage, and cleanup: useful, not compulsory

The machine has a steam wand. A pitcher is useful for steaming; a standalone electric frother is optional for a separate milk workflow, with potentially different texture. A knock box and mat improve cleanup. A puck screen may keep the shower screen cleaner, but needs appropriate basket headspace. Store beans airtight, away from heat and light; vacuum storage is optional. Buy quantities you will enjoy while fresh.

For the curious cook

Spend on control.
Not on mythology.

Think ingredients, mise en place, measurement, and taste. Espresso is a short, pressurized extraction; the machine is one part of the system.

Why the grinder gets so much of the budget

Grind size changes how water flows through the coffee bed. An espresso-capable grinder lets you make small adjustments instead of jumping between too fast and too slow. Good distribution and a level tamp still matter. The DF54 is my value choice here—not a claim that every grinder above it is wasted money.

Why the PRO fits this brief

I’m choosing a small footprint and low waiting time for everyday home use. Fast heating and switching serve that brief. Warm the cup and portafilter as part of preparation. This is a recommendation for a particular workflow, not proof that a compact budget machine equals every premium machine.

What spending more can genuinely buy

Depending on the model: temperature control, steam capacity, simultaneous brewing and steaming, easier back-to-back drinks, pressure profiling, or serviceable construction. Those can matter. My recommendation simply does not prioritize every capability for one or two classic-style drinks at home. Frequent entertaining or very light-roast experimentation may justify a different machine.

Why the recipe matters more than one magic number

Dose is the dry coffee weight; yield is the espresso weight in the cup. Changing their ratio changes concentration and flavor balance. Time is useful feedback, not a universal quality test. Keep your method consistent and let taste guide adjustments.

Why the basket and handle are different decisions

A single-wall basket relies on the coffee bed to provide resistance, so grind and preparation become central. A pressurized basket is more forgiving with less precisely ground coffee. Bottomless describes the handle, not the basket’s extraction quality: its open underside helps reveal uneven flow or spraying. Do not buy one expecting it to fix poor preparation.

Choose your destination

Classic or bright?
Neither is a mistake.

Billy’s preference

Classic Italian direction

Look for medium-dark or dark espresso blends described as chocolate, nuts, caramel, or molasses. Aim for sweetness, concentration, and body—not burnt bitterness. A useful starting ratio is 1:1.5 to 1:2 by weight.

A different expression

Lighter, brighter espresso

Lighter roasts can emphasize fruit, florals, and lively acidity. Try a starting ratio around 1:2 to 1:2.5, then adjust by taste. Third-wave coffee is broader than light roasting; these are flavor directions, not strict categories.

Darker roasts generally extract more readily. Lighter roasts often benefit from longer yields and, on machines that offer control, higher brew temperatures. These are starting principles, not guarantees about every bag. This recipe does not require a temperature adjustment on the PRO.

Your daily mise en place

Build a repeatable shot.

Start with a single-wall double basket and freshly ground coffee. Use the basket’s rated capacity: 14 g below is an example, not a universal dose. Follow your machine manual for operation and safe pressure release.

A starting recipe, sized to your basket

14 g coffee → 24.5 g espresso

Yield is grams of espresso, not milliliters. Roughly 20–35 seconds from pump start is a useful observation window for many conventional double shots, not a pass/fail test. Record time consistently; stop by yield and judge by taste.

  1. Warm and prepare.Use water suitable for the machine. Heat according to the manual and warm the cup and portafilter with a blank flush. Dry the basket before dosing.
  2. Weigh and grind.Use an appropriate basket dose. Grind just before brewing. Transfer carefully, break up clumps if needed, and distribute evenly.
  3. Tamp level.Press evenly and consistently. Avoid chasing an exact force. Leave appropriate headspace rather than packing grounds against the shower screen.
  4. Measure the shot.Place the cup on the scale and tare. Start brewing and timing; stop near the target yield, accounting for drips after stopping.
  5. Stir, taste, adjust one thing.Stir and cool slightly before tasting. Record dose, yield, time, and your next adjustment.
  6. Reset the station.Let pressure release as the manual directs before removing the handle; never force it. Empty and rinse the basket safely. Wipe and purge the steam wand after milk use. Follow the manual’s cleaning and descaling schedule.
Fast, thin, and sour?

Check uneven flow first. With a single-wall basket, grind a little finer while keeping dose and yield steady. If flow is even but the cup still tastes underdeveloped, try a slightly longer yield. Some brightness may be the coffee’s natural character.

Bitter, drying, or harsh?

Try a shorter yield. If the shot is also unusually slow, grind slightly coarser. Very dark roasting can itself taste bitter; recipe changes cannot entirely change a roast profile you dislike.

Choking or only dripping?

Stop brewing. After pressure safely releases, check for too much coffee, insufficient headspace, or an overly fine grind. Adjust one variable at a time. Do not force a pressurized handle loose.

Spraying or inconsistent shots?

A bottomless handle can reveal uneven flow. Check clumps, distribution, level tamping, and basket fit. Keep dose consistent and change only one variable per trial. More accessories are not automatically the answer.

Your best cup—not a perfect number.

Once it tastes sweet, balanced, and satisfying, save the recipe. Revisit it when the coffee changes. That is the useful kind of precision.
